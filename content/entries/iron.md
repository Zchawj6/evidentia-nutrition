## Bottom line

Iron supplementation has **strong, consistent evidence** for correcting iron deficiency and iron-deficiency anaemia.  
This benefit is **conditional on deficiency**.

In iron-replete individuals, supplementation **does not improve clinical outcomes** and exposes patients to avoidable risk.

---

## What the evidence shows

### Iron deficiency and iron-deficiency anaemia (Strong)

Across multiple RCTs and meta-analyses (human):

- Haemoglobin typically rises by **~10–20 g/L within 2–4 weeks** in adherent, responsive patients  
- Continued therapy restores iron stores (ferritin) over **6–12+ weeks**  
- Symptoms (fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance) improve in deficient individuals  

Effect sizes are large and clinically meaningful.

**Clinical nuance**
- Response depends on: baseline severity, adherence, ongoing blood loss, and cause of deficiency  
- Inflammation (via **hepcidin**) can blunt absorption and response  

---

### Fatigue in iron deficiency without anaemia (Moderate)

Human RCTs (limited, population-specific):

- **Modest improvements in fatigue** reported in iron-deficient, non-anaemic individuals  
- Evidence is strongest in **premenopausal women with low ferritin**

**Limitations**
- Smaller effect size than in anaemia  
- Heterogeneous trials; not generalisable to iron-replete adults  
- Some trials use fatigue scales with risk of subjective bias  

---

### Cognitive and physical performance (Moderate)

- Improvements seen in **iron-deficient children and adolescents** (human RCTs, meta-analyses)  
- **No consistent benefit** in iron-replete adults  

---

### Pregnancy (Strong)

Human evidence (Cochrane/meta-analyses):

- Reduces risk of **maternal anaemia at term**  
- Improves maternal iron status  

**Caveat**
- Effects on broader clinical outcomes (e.g. birth weight) are **context-dependent** and less consistent  

---

### Iron-replete individuals (Insufficient)

Human data:

- **No evidence of benefit** for fatigue, cognition, or general health  
- Routine supplementation is not evidence-based  

---

## Mechanism (supportive, not outcome-defining)

- Required for haemoglobin and oxygen transport  
- Central to mitochondrial oxidative metabolism  
- Involved in neurotransmitter synthesis  

**Constraint**
- Absorption regulated by **hepcidin** (liver-derived hormone)  
- High hepcidin → reduced absorption (e.g. inflammation, frequent dosing)

*Evidence type: mechanistic + human absorption studies*

---

## Defining deficiency (clinical thresholds)

Interpretation varies by context; commonly used ranges (human data/guidelines):

- **Ferritin <30 µg/L** → likely iron deficiency (high specificity in otherwise healthy adults)  
- **Ferritin 30–100 µg/L** → possible deficiency; interpret with CRP/inflammation and clinical context  
- **Ferritin >100 µg/L** → deficiency unlikely (unless significant inflammation)  

**Functional iron deficiency**
- Adequate stores but **impaired utilisation** (e.g. chronic disease, high hepcidin)  
- May respond poorly to oral iron; IV iron may be required in selected cases  

---

## Dosing and practical considerations

### Oral iron (human clinical use)

Typical:
- **40–100 mg elemental iron per dose**

**Dosing strategy**
- **Alternate-day dosing** can increase fractional absorption and reduce GI side effects (human crossover trials)  

**Administration**
- Better absorbed on an empty stomach  
- Reduced by: **calcium, phytates, tea, coffee**  
- Vitamin C: modest absorption benefit; limited clinical outcome evidence  

---

### Forms

- **Ferrous sulfate**: most studied; effective; higher GI side effects  
- **Ferrous fumarate / gluconate**: similar efficacy; tolerability varies  
- **Bisglycinate/chelates**: improved tolerability; fewer high-quality outcome trials  

*Evidence: human RCTs for ferrous salts; limited comparative outcome data for newer forms*

---

### Intravenous iron (clinical use)

Indicated when:

- Oral iron is ineffective or not tolerated  
- Malabsorption (e.g. IBD, post-surgery)  
- **Functional iron deficiency** (inflammation, CKD, heart failure contexts)  
- Rapid repletion required  

*Evidence: human RCTs and guideline-based practice*

---

## Interactions and absorption modifiers

**Reduce absorption**
- Calcium supplements  
- Proton pump inhibitors  
- Tea, coffee (polyphenols)  
- High-phytate foods  

**Clinical implication**
- Timing and co-administration materially affect effectiveness  

---

## Safety and risks

### Common (human RCTs and observational)

- Nausea  
- Constipation or diarrhoea  
- Abdominal discomfort  

These are dose-dependent and limit adherence.

---

### Serious / context-specific

- Iron overload in susceptible individuals (e.g. haemochromatosis)  
- Accidental overdose (especially in children)  
- Caution in:
  - Active infection (iron may support pathogen growth in some contexts)  
  - Chronic inflammatory states  

---

### Intravenous iron

- Effective and generally safe in appropriate settings  
- Rare hypersensitivity reactions  

---

## Interpretation

Iron is a **status-dependent intervention**:

- **Deficient → large, reliable benefit (high certainty)**  
- **Replete → no benefit (moderate–high certainty)**  

This is one of the clearest examples in nutrition where:
> baseline biology determines effect

---

## Practical application

Iron supplementation should be:

- **Test-guided (ferritin ± CRP)**  
- Targeted to:
  - confirmed deficiency  
  - high-risk groups (e.g. pregnancy, heavy menstrual loss)  
- Monitored:
  - haemoglobin (2–4 weeks)  
  - ferritin (longer-term repletion)  

Avoid routine use without evidence of deficiency.

---

## Evidence summary by type

**Human RCTs / meta-analyses**
- Strong for anaemia correction  
- Moderate for fatigue in selected non-anaemic populations  
- Moderate for cognitive outcomes in deficient children  

**Mechanistic / physiology**
- Hepcidin regulation explains dosing strategy and variability  

**Observational**
- Used mainly for safety context; not relied upon for efficacy claims
