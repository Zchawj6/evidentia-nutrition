## What it is

Resveratrol is a polyphenol compound found naturally in the skin of red grapes, certain berries, and peanuts. It is produced by plants in response to stress, injury, and fungal infection. The compound attracted significant scientific and popular attention following observations that it extended lifespan in yeast, worms, flies, and mice -- and that it appeared to activate sirtuins, a family of proteins associated with longevity pathways.

The commercial form is trans-resveratrol, typically derived from Japanese knotweed (Polygonum cuspidatum) rather than grape sources, at concentrations many times higher than any dietary intake could provide. This distinction is relevant: the concentrations used in preclinical research and commercial supplements bear little resemblance to those achievable through food.

## What the evidence shows

The resveratrol story is one of the most instructive cases in supplement science of how promising preclinical findings can fail to replicate in humans -- and of how the commercial narrative can accelerate far ahead of the actual evidence. Where effects are observed in human trials, they are generally small and inconsistently reproduced across studies.

**Longevity and lifespan extension** in humans has not been demonstrated. The animal data -- particularly Howitz et al. (2003) showing lifespan extension in yeast and the subsequent work in mice by Baur et al. (2006) -- generated enormous excitement. These findings have not translated into human trials of meaningful duration or outcomes. No human trial has assessed longevity endpoints directly, and surrogate markers associated with longevity pathways have shown inconsistent responses.

**Cardiovascular protection** was one of the most heavily promoted applications, partly tied to the "French paradox" hypothesis that red wine consumption might explain lower rates of cardiovascular disease in France despite a rich diet. Human trials examining blood pressure, lipids, endothelial function, and inflammatory markers have returned inconsistent results. Some meta-analyses report modest systolic blood pressure reductions at higher doses (above 300mg/day), but these effects are not consistent across trials and do not reach clinical decisiveness. A Cochrane-level systematic review would not currently support a cardiovascular benefit claim.

**Metabolic health** represents the most modest positive signal in the literature. Some trials in individuals with type 2 diabetes or metabolic syndrome have reported improvements in insulin sensitivity and fasting glucose, but effect sizes are small, durations short, and replication incomplete. This is the one area where an emerging signal exists -- it does not justify a broad metabolic health claim.

**SIRT1 activation and longevity pathway modulation** -- the central mechanistic claim -- has not been consistently demonstrated in humans at commercially relevant doses. The foundational paper linking resveratrol to SIRT1 activation (Howitz et al., 2003) was followed by significant controversy when Pfizer researchers reported that the original assay used a fluorescent tag that produced artefactual activation results. Subsequent work with more rigorous methods produced substantially smaller and less consistent effects.

## Why the preclinical evidence did not translate

This is worth understanding in detail, because it illustrates a general principle relevant to many supplements.

**Dose and bioavailability.** Oral resveratrol is rapidly metabolised and conjugated in the gut and liver. Peak plasma concentrations following typical supplement doses (150--500mg) are low, and the compound is largely converted to sulphate and glucuronide metabolites before reaching systemic circulation. The free resveratrol concentrations achievable in humans are substantially lower than those used in cell culture experiments. Modified delivery forms (micronised, liposomal, combined with piperine) have been developed to address this, but robust clinical outcome data for these forms remains limited. There is also an unresolved question about whether sulphate and glucuronide metabolites retain biological activity in tissues following deconjugation -- this possibility means the "low free plasma level" argument against efficacy is not fully settled, though it does not rescue the clinical evidence picture. This creates a mismatch between doses that may be required for meaningful biological activity and doses that are practical and tolerable in humans. Variation in formulation and bioavailability across trials further limits comparability of the existing evidence base.

**Species differences.** The metabolic and genetic context in which resveratrol activates longevity pathways differs substantially between yeast, worms, flies, mice, and humans. Lifespan extension in short-lived organisms with simple metabolic regulation does not reliably predict effects in humans with very different longevity biology.

**The SIRT1 assay controversy.** The original high-profile finding linking resveratrol to SIRT1 activation was subsequently challenged on methodological grounds. While resveratrol does interact with SIRT1 under some conditions, the magnitude and consistency of this effect in humans at achievable concentrations is far more modest than the early literature suggested. The field has moved on: current understanding positions resveratrol's effects -- where they exist -- as likely indirect, potentially AMPK-mediated or context-dependent, rather than as direct, potent SIRT1 activation. The assay artefact was one contributor to the translation failure, not the whole explanation.

**Confounded epidemiology.** The French paradox hypothesis -- that resveratrol in red wine explains a cardiovascular advantage -- has been largely undermined. Cohort data on urinary resveratrol metabolites, which provide a more objective measure of actual exposure than dietary recall, has not shown a consistent association with cardiovascular outcomes. The epidemiological signal, where it exists, is more plausibly explained by overall dietary pattern, lifestyle, and alcohol-related confounding than by resveratrol specifically.

## Five questions

### Does resveratrol correct a deficiency state?

No. Resveratrol is not an essential nutrient and there is no recognised deficiency state. Dietary intake from grape products is low and variable; the body does not require exogenous resveratrol to maintain any known physiological function. This question does not apply.

### Does supplementation prevent disease in at-risk populations?

No robust evidence supports this. No long-duration prevention trial has been conducted, and no RCTs with hard clinical endpoints such as cardiovascular events or mortality exist. The mechanistic rationale for cardiovascular or metabolic disease prevention was based on preclinical data that has not been replicated in human trials of sufficient size or duration to assess disease endpoints. The evidence does not currently support a prevention claim in any population.

### Does resveratrol produce meaningful biomarker effects?

Inconsistently and without a clear direction. Across trials examining blood pressure, lipid profiles, inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6), endothelial function, and insulin sensitivity, results are mixed. Some trials report modest improvements in individual markers; others report no effect or worsening. No biomarker response is consistent enough to serve as a reliable indicator of biological activity at commonly used doses. SIRT1 and related longevity pathway markers have not shown consistent activation in human studies at commercially relevant doses.

### Does resveratrol improve outcomes in clinical populations?

There is a modest and inconsistent signal in metabolic populations -- particularly individuals with type 2 diabetes or insulin resistance -- for improvements in fasting glucose and insulin sensitivity. Effect sizes are small, trial durations are short (typically 8--16 weeks), and independent replication is limited. This is the most credible clinical signal in the current literature. It does not support a broad therapeutic claim.

### Does resveratrol benefit healthy, replete adults?

The evidence does not support this. The theoretical longevity and anti-ageing rationale has not translated into demonstrable benefit in healthy populations in any well-conducted human trial. The marketing proposition for healthy adults -- that resveratrol activates longevity pathways and extends healthspan -- rests almost entirely on preclinical data that has not been reproduced under human conditions.

## Biomarker and surrogate considerations

No validated surrogate for resveratrol's proposed clinical effects exists in humans. SIRT1 activity, NAD+ levels, and related markers have been used as exploratory endpoints in some trials, but none are validated surrogates for clinical outcomes. Plasma resveratrol concentrations are measurable but do not reliably predict biological effect given the rapid metabolism and conjugation of the compound. Interpreting a "detectable blood level" as confirmation of biological activity is not supported by the evidence.

**outcomeType: exploratory-biomarker** (no validated surrogate established)

## Individual variation

There is no well-characterised population for whom resveratrol supplementation produces consistent and clinically meaningful benefit. The metabolic signal in individuals with type 2 diabetes and insulin resistance is the closest candidate, but the evidence is too inconsistent and the effect sizes too small to support targeted recommendations in this group at present.

Genetic variation in resveratrol metabolism (particularly CYP1B1 and sulphotransferase activity) may influence bioavailability, but this has not been translated into clinically useful guidance.

## Safety

Resveratrol is generally well-tolerated at doses up to 1g/day in short-term trials. GI discomfort -- nausea, loose stools, abdominal pain -- is the most commonly reported side effect and appears dose-dependent.

At higher doses (2.5g/day and above), reversible elevations in liver enzymes have been reported in some trials, including a study in multiple myeloma patients. This does not establish hepatotoxicity at standard supplement doses but warrants caution at very high doses or in individuals with pre-existing liver conditions.

Resveratrol inhibits CYP3A4 and CYP2C9 enzymes and may increase plasma concentrations of drugs metabolised by these pathways, including some anticoagulants, NSAIDs, and statins. This is clinically relevant and should be considered before use alongside these medications.

In vitro oestrogenic activity has been demonstrated. The clinical significance in humans is uncertain, but caution is warranted in individuals with oestrogen-sensitive conditions or those on hormone therapies.

## Funding and independence

The resveratrol evidence base has been substantially shaped by commercial interest following the early preclinical excitement. GlaxoSmithKline acquired Sirtris Pharmaceuticals -- a company founded on the SIRT1/resveratrol hypothesis -- for $720 million in 2008, before subsequently discontinuing the resveratrol programme after trials failed to demonstrate clinical efficacy. This is illustrative of the translation gap between preclinical promise and human evidence, not in itself proof of inefficacy -- companies discontinue programmes for many reasons including commercial rather than purely scientific ones. The more meaningful signal is that the discontinuation aligned with a broader pattern of null or inconsistent human trial results across multiple independent research groups.

Several academic trials have been independently funded, but the field as a whole has been subject to significant publication bias toward positive findings.
