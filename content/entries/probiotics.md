## What it is

Probiotics are live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host. This definition, established by the WHO and FAO, contains two critical qualifiers that are frequently ignored in consumer marketing: adequacy of amount and demonstrated benefit. Not every live culture product meets either criterion.

The most commonly supplemented probiotic genera are Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, both of which include dozens of species and hundreds of strains. Saccharomyces boulardii, a yeast rather than a bacterium, is the most studied non-bacterial probiotic and has a distinct evidence base from bacterial strains. The genus and species designation -- Lactobacillus acidophilus, for example -- does not determine clinical effect. The strain designation does. This is the most important and most routinely ignored principle in probiotic evaluation.

The gut microbiome is a complex ecosystem of trillions of microorganisms whose composition varies substantially between individuals, changes with diet, age, antibiotics, and illness, and whose relationship to health outcomes is incompletely understood. Probiotic supplementation adds specific live organisms to this ecosystem. Whether those organisms survive transit, colonise even temporarily, and produce meaningful effects depends on strain characteristics, dose, delivery format, the individual's existing microbiome, and the specific outcome being considered.

## The strain-specificity principle

This is the foundational concept for interpreting any probiotic evidence and the point at which most consumer-facing probiotic content fails.

Evidence generated for Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG in antibiotic-associated diarrhoea does not apply to a product containing Lactobacillus acidophilus. Evidence for Saccharomyces boulardii in traveller's diarrhoea does not apply to a Bifidobacterium combination. Evidence for a specific multi-strain combination in IBS does not apply to a different multi-strain combination with different organisms at different doses.

This is not a theoretical concern. It is the reason that systematic reviews of probiotics frequently produce inconsistent results -- they pool evidence across strains and indications that should not be pooled. It is also the reason that a product label showing a high CFU count of multiple strains tells a consumer almost nothing about what clinical effect, if any, that product is likely to produce.

When evaluating any probiotic product or claim, the relevant questions are: which specific strain was studied, in which population, for which outcome, at what dose, and has it been independently replicated?

## What the evidence shows

**Antibiotic-associated diarrhoea prevention.** This is the most robustly generalisable probiotic application and the only one where a moderate general recommendation can be made across strain types. Multiple meta-analyses, including a Cochrane review of 39 trials, consistently show that probiotics reduce the incidence of antibiotic-associated diarrhoea by approximately 50% compared to placebo. This effect holds across several different strains, which is what justifies a category-level rather than strain-specific recommendation here. Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG and Saccharomyces boulardii have the strongest individual evidence bases within this indication.

**IBS symptom relief.** Multiple trials show symptom improvement with specific probiotic strains in IBS, particularly for bloating, abdominal pain, and bowel habit irregularity. The evidence is genuine but strain-dependent -- it does not support choosing any probiotic for IBS. The high placebo response rate in IBS trials, often 30 to 40%, means that poorly blinded studies substantially overestimate probiotic effects. Specific strains with the strongest IBS evidence are covered in individual strain entries.

**C. difficile prevention.** Probiotics reduce C. difficile-associated diarrhoea in hospitalised patients receiving antibiotics. The effect is meaningful in high-risk hospital settings but the evidence does not support community-level prophylactic use.

**Immune and respiratory outcomes.** Some strain-specific trials show modest reductions in upper respiratory infection frequency or duration. This is not a class effect and should not be interpreted as general immune support evidence for probiotics as a category. Immune biomarker changes are frequently cited in probiotic marketing but do not reliably predict infection outcomes.

**Gut-brain axis and mental health.** The gut-brain axis is a legitimate and active research area. Clinical evidence for mood or mental health benefit from probiotic supplementation is currently insufficient. Psychobiotic claims are premature.

## The five questions

### Does low status cause harm that supplementation corrects?

Not in the way this applies to nutrient deficiencies. Dysbiosis -- disruption of the gut microbiome -- is associated with various health conditions, but the relationship is complex, bidirectional, and not well-enough characterised to identify a correctable deficit state in the way that applies to vitamin D or zinc deficiency. Antibiotic-associated disruption of the microbiome is the clearest context where probiotic repletion has demonstrated benefit.

### Does supplementation prevent disease in at-risk populations?

Yes, in specific contexts. Antibiotic-associated diarrhoea and C. difficile infection in high-risk hospitalised patients are the clearest prevention applications. Outside these contexts, prevention evidence is strain-specific and insufficiently established for general recommendations.

### Does probiotic supplementation produce meaningful biomarker effects?

Yes, across several systems -- microbiome composition, immune markers, inflammatory markers, and intestinal permeability markers all respond to probiotic supplementation in various trials. These are mostly exploratory biomarker findings. Microbiome composition changes are not validated clinical surrogates for any specific health outcome. The relationship between microbiome changes and clinical benefit is assumed more often than it is demonstrated.

### Does probiotic supplementation improve outcomes in clinical populations?

Yes, in well-defined and strain-specific contexts: antibiotic-associated diarrhoea, IBS with specific strains, C. difficile prevention in high-risk patients, and specific urogenital applications. Beyond these contexts the evidence is early-stage or insufficient.

### Does probiotic supplementation benefit healthy, replete adults?

The evidence is limited and strain-specific. Some healthy population trials show modest benefits for digestive comfort, immune resilience, and respiratory infection frequency with specific strains. General probiotic supplementation in healthy adults without a specific indication is not well-supported by the current evidence base.

## Understanding probiotic products

**CFU count** -- colony forming units -- is the most commonly marketed metric but one of the least informative for predicting clinical effect. The relevant questions are which strains are present, whether they are viable at the point of consumption, and whether those specific strains have been studied for the relevant indication.

**Viability** is affected by heat, moisture, and time. Many probiotic products do not maintain their labelled CFU counts to the end of shelf life. Refrigerated storage generally maintains viability better than ambient storage, though some strains are formulated for ambient stability.

**Delivery matrix** matters. Enteric-coated capsules may protect strains through stomach acid transit better than standard capsules, though evidence on the clinical significance of delivery format differences is limited.

**Multi-strain combinations** are not inherently superior to single strains. The evidence base for most multi-strain products is weaker than for well-characterised single strains like Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, because the combination itself is rarely studied as a whole.

## Safety

Probiotics are safe for the large majority of healthy individuals. Transient bloating and GI discomfort at initiation are the most commonly reported effects and typically resolve within one to two weeks. Serious adverse events are rare but documented -- bacteraemia from Lactobacillus strains and fungaemia from Saccharomyces boulardii have been reported in immunocompromised patients, critically ill individuals, and those with compromised gut barrier function. These populations should not use probiotic supplements without medical supervision.

Product quality and strain authenticity are practical concerns. The supplement probiotic market is not consistently regulated for strain identity or viability. Third-party testing from reputable organisations provides the most reliable verification.

## What can reasonably be concluded

Probiotics have genuine evidence for specific, well-defined applications. Antibiotic-associated diarrhoea prevention is the most robustly generalisable, and specific strains have meaningful evidence for IBS, C. difficile prevention, and urogenital health. These are real clinical effects and should not be dismissed.

The problem is not that probiotics do not work. It is that the evidence is strain-specific and indication-specific, and this principle is systematically ignored in commercial probiotic marketing, which presents high CFU counts and long strain lists as evidence of broad health benefit. A product cannot be evaluated by its label alone -- the relevant question is always which strain, for which indication, in which population.

Individual strain entries on this site address specific organisms and their evidence bases in depth. Where evidence is limited or outcomes are uncertain, conclusions should be treated as provisional and subject to revision as the evidence base develops.

## Evidence base by outcome

### Antibiotic-associated diarrhoea

Hempel S et al. (2012, JAMA; meta-analysis of 82 RCTs, n = 11,811) found that probiotics were associated with a significant reduction in antibiotic-associated diarrhoea (RR 0.58, 95% CI 0.50 to 0.68), representing approximately a 42% reduction in risk. The effect was present across multiple strain types, which is the key finding justifying a category-level rather than strain-specific recommendation for this indication.

Goldenberg JZ et al. (2013, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews; 63 RCTs, n = 11,811) confirmed this finding and noted that Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG and Saccharomyces boulardii had the strongest individual evidence bases. The number needed to treat was approximately 13, meaning one case of antibiotic-associated diarrhoea prevented for every 13 patients given probiotics.

Timing matters -- probiotics initiated at the same time as the antibiotic course show stronger effects than those started after diarrhoea develops.

**Evidence rating basis:** Moderate. Consistent effect across multiple strains and large meta-analyses. The cross-strain consistency is what justifies the Moderate rating at the category level for this indication specifically.

### IBS

Ford AC et al. (2014, American Journal of Gastroenterology; meta-analysis of 43 RCTs, n = 3,452) found that probiotics were superior to placebo for global IBS symptoms and abdominal pain, but noted substantial heterogeneity across trials. The overall RR for persistent symptoms was 0.79 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.89).

The high placebo response rate in IBS is a critical confounding factor. In well-blinded trials, probiotic effects are smaller than in poorly blinded ones. The practical implication is that the pooled effect size in meta-analyses likely overstates the true benefit from well-characterised strains in real-world use.

**Evidence rating basis:** Moderate (strain-dependent). The category-level signal is consistent enough to support a Moderate rating, but clinical application requires strain selection based on specific evidence rather than choosing any probiotic product.

### C. difficile prevention

Goldenberg JZ et al. (2017, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews; 31 RCTs, n = 8,672) found that probiotics reduced C. difficile-associated diarrhoea by approximately 60% in high-risk hospitalised patients (RR 0.40, 95% CI 0.30 to 0.52). The effect was most pronounced in trials where baseline C. difficile incidence was above 5%, suggesting greatest benefit in high-risk settings.

**Evidence rating basis:** Moderate for high-risk hospitalised populations. Not established for community antibiotic use.

### Gut-brain axis

The gut-brain axis describes bidirectional communication between the enteric nervous system and the central nervous system, mediated via vagal signalling, immune pathways, and microbial metabolites including short-chain fatty acids and neurotransmitter precursors. The mechanistic rationale for psychobiotic effects is plausible and actively researched.

Human clinical trial evidence remains limited. Dinan TG et al. and subsequent researchers have proposed that specific strains may modulate stress responses and mood via gut-brain pathways, but adequately powered clinical trials in human populations are scarce and results are inconsistent.

**Evidence rating basis:** Insufficient for clinical application. Mechanistic rationale is scientifically interesting; clinical translation is not yet established.
