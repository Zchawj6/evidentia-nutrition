## What it is

Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) is a root extract from Ayurvedic medicine with increasing use in Western supplement markets. The active constituents are believed to include withanolides, though the relative contribution of individual compounds to observed effects has not been established. Most clinical evidence uses one of two proprietary standardised extracts: KSM-66 (root-only, standardised to at least 5% withanolides) or Sensoril (root and leaf, standardised to at least 10% withanolides). Evidence from these trials should not be assumed to apply to non-standardised or generic products, which vary considerably in active constituent content.

A significant proportion of the trial evidence is industry-funded by the manufacturers of KSM-66 or Sensoril. Effect sizes in independent trials are systematically smaller than in industry-funded work. This limitation runs through the evidence base and is reflected throughout this entry.

## What the evidence shows

**Stress reduction** is the most consistently supported outcome. Multiple randomised controlled trials using KSM-66 or Sensoril at doses of 300-600mg/day demonstrate reductions in self-reported stress on validated rating scales including the Perceived Stress Scale. Effect sizes across trials are in the range of SMD 0.4-0.7, which is moderate by conventional thresholds but should be interpreted with caution given the high proportion of industry-funded work and short trial durations of 6-12 weeks. The signal is real; the magnitude is uncertain.

**Sleep quality** in individuals with baseline sleep disturbance shows a consistent directional signal across several trials. Improvements in sleep onset latency, total sleep time, and sleep efficiency have been reported. Effects are most consistent in individuals with self-reported sleep problems at baseline; evidence in good sleepers is limited.

**Cortisol** reductions of approximately 15-30% from baseline have been reported in stressed populations. These estimates vary widely across trials and are sensitive to measurement methodology, assay type, and timing of sampling. Cortisol is best understood here as an exploratory biomarker, a consistent directional finding in stressed populations, but with uncertain clinical translation.

## What the evidence does not show

Physical performance effects are substantially smaller and less consistent than established ergogenic aids such as creatine or caffeine. The available evidence does not support ashwagandha as a meaningful performance supplement by current standards.

Cognitive effects vary widely depending on the task and population studied. The evidence is too heterogeneous and too reliant on industry-funded small trials to support confident claims.

Testosterone effects in healthy men with normal baseline testosterone are small and inconsistently replicated. The more consistent signal is in sub-fertile men or those with low baseline testosterone, where modest improvements in sperm parameters and hormone levels have been reported.

## Extract type matters

Evidence from KSM-66 or Sensoril trials is not transferable to non-standardised ashwagandha without direct comparative data. Many consumer products use generic root powder with variable withanolide content at uncertain doses. Where a specific outcome is the goal, product selection should match the extract type and dose used in the relevant evidence base.

## Who the evidence applies to

The evidence is most consistent in individuals with genuinely elevated baseline stress or sleep disturbance. Those with low stress or good sleep are less likely to show measurable benefit.

The testosterone and fertility evidence applies most clearly to sub-fertile men or those with documented hormonal concerns. Evidence in healthy men with normal parameters is insufficient.

Women in perimenopause and menopause represent an emerging relevant population, with early trial evidence for stress, sleep, and symptom relief in this life stage.

## Safety and contraindications

Ashwagandha is generally well-tolerated at doses used in clinical trials. However, rare but serious cases of liver injury have been reported, with incidence unknown due to the absence of denominator data. Any symptoms of liver dysfunction, jaundice, abdominal pain, dark urine, should prompt discontinuation and clinical assessment. Individuals with pre-existing liver conditions should not use ashwagandha without medical supervision.

Ashwagandha is contraindicated in pregnancy due to traditional use as a uterine stimulant. It should not be used during breastfeeding without clinical review.

Rare cases of clinically significant thyroid effects have been reported. Individuals with hyperthyroidism or those taking thyroid medication should seek clinical advice before use. Ashwagandha may potentiate sedative medications. Caution is appropriate in autoimmune conditions.

## What can reasonably be concluded

Ashwagandha has a genuine and growing evidence base for stress reduction and sleep quality improvement, particularly in individuals with elevated baseline stress or sleep disturbance. The Moderate rating reflects a real signal, but confidence is reduced by systematic industry funding bias, small trial sizes, and short durations.

Extract standardisation matters: KSM-66 and Sensoril are the evidence-based choices. Non-standardised products cannot be assumed equivalent.

The safety profile is generally acceptable at normal doses but includes rare serious risks, particularly hepatotoxicity, that are often absent from commercial communications and should be part of any honest assessment.

Where evidence is limited or outcomes are uncertain, conclusions should be treated as provisional and subject to revision as the evidence base develops.

## Evidence base by outcome

### Stress reduction

Multiple randomised controlled trials using KSM-66 or Sensoril at 300-600mg/day demonstrate reductions in self-reported stress on validated scales. The most cited include Chandrasekhar et al. (2012), which used 300mg KSM-66 twice daily and found significant reductions on the PSS and GHQ-28 over 60 days, and a meta-analysis by Pratte et al. (2014) that pooled available trial data and found consistent directional effects.

Effect sizes across trials are approximately SMD 0.4-0.7, moderate by conventional thresholds. However, these figures are drawn predominantly from industry-funded trials with small samples and short durations. Independent trials tend to show smaller effect sizes. The true population-level effect, corrected for funding bias, is likely at the lower end of this range.

Trial durations are almost uniformly 6-12 weeks. Long-term effects beyond 3 months are not well-characterised. Most stress measures are subjective self-report scales, which are susceptible to expectancy effects and are harder to blind effectively than objective endpoints.

**Evidence rating basis:** Moderate (reduced confidence). Signal is consistent and directional. Magnitude uncertain; confidence reduced by systematic bias, small samples, and short duration.

### Sleep quality

Langade et al. (2019) used 300mg KSM-66 twice daily in adults with insomnia and found significant improvements in sleep onset latency, total sleep time, and sleep efficiency measured by actigraphy, an objective endpoint, which strengthens this finding relative to the purely subjective stress data. Chandrasekhar et al. (2012) found sleep quality improvements as a secondary outcome.

A meta-analysis by Cheah et al. (2021) pooled five trials and found significant improvements across sleep quality measures. Effects were strongest in participants with self-reported sleep problems at baseline. Evidence in good sleepers is limited and less consistent.

**Evidence rating basis:** Moderate (reduced confidence). The actigraphy data in Langade et al. is a relative strength. Evidence most applicable to those with baseline sleep disturbance.

### Cortisol

Several trials report cortisol reductions of approximately 15-30% from baseline in stressed populations. This directional finding is consistent, but the estimates vary widely and are sensitive to: time of day of measurement, assay methodology (serum vs salivary vs urinary), whether single or multiple time points are used, and regression to mean in stressed populations at baseline.

Cortisol is an exploratory biomarker here. It does not have the validated surrogate status that would allow confident inference about clinical outcomes from biomarker change alone. The cortisol data is consistent with the stress and sleep clinical data but should not be read as independent evidence of a distinct effect.

Mechanistic pathways proposed include HPA axis modulation, GABAergic activity, and effects on stress-response gene expression. None of these has been definitively established in humans. These remain speculative in humans and should not be allowed to carry interpretive weight beyond the clinical outcome data.

**outcomeType: exploratory-biomarker**

### Testosterone and male fertility

In sub-fertile men or those with documented low testosterone, several small trials have reported improvements in sperm count, motility, and morphology, and modest increases in serum testosterone of approximately 10-20% above baseline. Mahdi et al. (2009) and Ambiye et al. (2013) are the most cited references.

These effects are small and most apparent in men with low baseline testosterone or fertility concerns. Evidence in healthy men with normal parameters is insufficient to support a meaningful claim. Trials are small (typically under 60 participants), short-term, and predominantly industry-funded. Independent replication at adequate power is lacking.

**Evidence rating basis:** Emerging for sub-fertile men or those with low baseline testosterone. Insufficient for healthy men with normal parameters.

### Physical performance

Several trials report improvements in VO2 max, muscle strength, and recovery, including Wankhede et al. (2015). Effect sizes are small to moderate in the available trials, but these are predominantly small, short, and industry-funded. The effects reported are substantially smaller and less consistent than established ergogenic aids. Ashwagandha should not be positioned as a meaningful performance supplement by current evidence standards.

**Evidence rating basis:** Emerging. Not comparable to creatine or caffeine in evidence strength or effect size.

### Cognitive function

A small number of trials report improvements in reaction time, memory, and executive function. Choudhary et al. (2017) found improvements in adults with mild cognitive impairment. Evidence in healthy adults is less consistent. Outcomes vary widely depending on the cognitive task and population studied, making cross-trial comparison difficult. The evidence base is small, heterogeneous, and predominantly industry-funded.

**Evidence rating basis:** Emerging. Too few adequately powered independent trials to support a confident claim.

### Women across life stages

**Perimenopause and menopause** represent an emerging and clinically relevant application. The hormonal fluctuations of perimenopause are associated with increased stress reactivity, sleep disturbance, anxiety, cognitive changes, and vasomotor symptoms. Ashwagandha's proposed mechanism via HPA axis modulation is biologically relevant to this transition.

A randomised controlled trial by Dongre et al. (2015) in perimenopausal women found improvements in menopausal symptom scores, sexual function, and psychological wellbeing with KSM-66 supplementation. A trial by Gopal et al. (2021) in climacteric women found reductions in menopausal symptom severity including hot flushes, sleep disturbance, and anxiety. Trial sizes are small and replication is limited, but the population relevance is high and the direction of findings is consistent.

This is a meaningful personalisation consideration for women in perimenopause and early menopause experiencing stress, sleep disturbance, or anxiety symptoms. The evidence is at an earlier stage than the general stress and sleep literature but is developing positively and deserves to be highlighted given the relevance to this life stage.

**Fertility in women:** The evidence base here is very limited. There is early preclinical work on ashwagandha's role in female reproductive health, but no adequately powered clinical trials currently support recommendations around female fertility supplementation with ashwagandha.

**Pregnancy:** Ashwagandha is contraindicated in pregnancy due to traditional evidence of uterotonic activity. This is not a population where supplementation should be considered.

**Thyroid relevance for women:** Thyroid conditions are significantly more prevalent in women than men. Some trials have reported small increases in T3 and T4 with ashwagandha supplementation. Rare cases of clinically significant thyroid effects have been reported. This makes the thyroid caution particularly relevant for women, who are more likely to have pre-existing thyroid conditions or subclinical thyroid dysfunction.

**Stress, HPA axis, and hormonal interactions:** Women experience higher rates of stress-related conditions including generalised anxiety and burnout, and HPA axis dysregulation interacts with oestrogen and progesterone in ways that may make women more responsive to HPA-modulating interventions. This is mechanistically plausible but has not been specifically tested in adequately powered sex-stratified trials.

The overall picture for women is that ashwagandha's core evidence domains, stress and sleep, are directly relevant to common concerns at multiple life stages. The perimenopausal and menopausal evidence, while early, is promising and particularly well-matched to the proposed mechanism. Women with thyroid conditions require specific caution.

## Individual variation

**Baseline stress and cortisol:** The evidence is most consistent in individuals with genuinely elevated stress. Low-stress individuals are less likely to show meaningful benefit. Baseline stress load is the most relevant individual factor.

**Sleep:** Evidence most applicable to those with baseline sleep disturbance. Benefit in good sleepers is not established.

**Extract type and dose:** Response will vary substantially depending on which extract is used and at what dose. KSM-66 and Sensoril have different withanolide profiles and standardisation levels. Matching product to the relevant evidence base matters.

**Hormonal status:** The testosterone and fertility evidence is most applicable to men with sub-optimal baseline parameters. Women in perimenopause and menopause represent a specific relevant population for stress, sleep, and symptom outcomes.

**Thyroid status:** Individuals with thyroid conditions, particularly more prevalent in women, should exercise caution and seek clinical advice before use.

## Funding and independence

Industry funding dominance is the most significant methodological issue in the ashwagandha literature. The majority of clinical trials involve the manufacturers of KSM-66 (Ixoreal Biomed) or Sensoril (Natreon). Effect sizes in independent trials are consistently smaller. This does not invalidate the evidence but means the most commonly cited trial results likely overstate the real-world effect. Independent, adequately powered replication of the core stress and sleep findings would substantially strengthen confidence in this evidence base.
