## What it is

Omega-3 fatty acids -- principally eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) -- are long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids with roles in cardiovascular function, inflammatory regulation, and neurodevelopment. The body cannot synthesise EPA and DHA efficiently from shorter-chain precursors, making dietary or supplemental intake the primary source for most people.

The main dietary sources are oily fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines, herring) and, for those avoiding fish, algal oil -- the original source of EPA and DHA in the marine food chain. Regular oily fish consumption provides meaningful EPA and DHA; individuals with little or no fish intake are most likely to have low omega-3 status.

## What the evidence shows

**Triglyceride reduction** is the best-supported cardiovascular biomarker effect. High-dose EPA/DHA (2-4g/day) reliably reduces fasting triglycerides by 20-30% in individuals with elevated baseline levels. This effect is dose-dependent and well-replicated. It is a strong biomarker effect; clinical outcome relevance is uncertain and should not be assumed from the biomarker change alone.

**Omega-3 index correction** in individuals with low status is consistent and well-evidenced. Supplementation reliably raises the omega-3 index (EPA + DHA as a percentage of red blood cell fatty acids) in a dose-dependent manner. The index is most useful as a risk stratification and monitoring tool; it should not be treated as a validated treatment target with a proven relationship to clinical outcomes.

**Cardiovascular event prevention** through standard supplement use is inconsistent across major trials. REDUCE-IT (2019) showed benefit with pharmaceutical-dose EPA-only, but this finding is complicated by a controversial mineral oil placebo and is not generalisable to combined EPA/DHA supplements. STRENGTH (2020) using combined EPA/DHA at the same dose was null. VITAL (2019) showed a modest signal with 1g/day in those with low fish intake. The biological reason for the EPA-only versus EPA/DHA divergence remains unresolved.

**DHA in pregnancy and early life** is supported by evidence for the importance of adequate DHA intake during fetal development and lactation. Algal DHA is accepted as bioequivalent to fish-derived DHA for this purpose in multiple regulatory contexts.

**Depression** as an adjunct treatment with EPA-dominant preparations shows a modest, consistent signal in diagnosed populations. Effect sizes are small to moderate and sensitive to study selection and publication bias.

## What the evidence does not show

The evidence does not support general omega-3 supplementation for healthy adults with adequate dietary intake. Cognitive enhancement, general anti-inflammatory benefit, and mood improvement in replete individuals without clinical indication are not consistently demonstrated in adequately powered trials.

The cardiovascular claims that dominate omega-3 marketing substantially outrun what the current trial evidence supports for supplement-dose preparations.

## Form and dose considerations

Algal oil provides EPA and DHA without the fish supply chain and is the primary choice for those avoiding animal products. Bioavailability of algal DHA is well-established as comparable to fish oil DHA. Clinical outcome equivalence between algal and fish oil has not been established in large trials and should not be assumed from bioavailability data alone.

EPA:DHA ratios vary across algal products. EPA and DHA have partially distinct biological roles -- EPA more strongly associated with triglyceride reduction and inflammatory modulation; DHA with structural neural and retinal roles. Evidence from one EPA:DHA profile should not be extrapolated to products with materially different ratios.

Triglyceride-lowering effects require 2-4g/day. General dietary adequacy and omega-3 index correction can be achieved at lower doses in most individuals.

## Who the evidence applies to

The evidence is most consistent for individuals with low omega-3 status -- particularly those with minimal oily fish intake including vegetarians and vegans -- and for specific populations including pregnant women, those with elevated triglycerides, and individuals with diagnosed depression using EPA-dominant preparations.

Healthy adults with adequate dietary intake of oily fish are unlikely to benefit meaningfully from additional supplementation. The appropriate starting point is assessment of dietary intake and, where feasible, measurement of the omega-3 index.

## Safety and contraindications

Omega-3 is generally well-tolerated at standard supplement doses. At doses of 3-4g/day, there is increased bleeding risk, particularly with anticoagulant medications. An atrial fibrillation signal was observed in both REDUCE-IT and STRENGTH at 4g/day; this has not been consistently shown at lower supplement doses. GI effects including fishy aftertaste and loose stools are common and reduced by taking with food. High-dose DHA-dominant preparations may modestly raise LDL-C in some individuals.

## What can reasonably be concluded

Omega-3 supplementation has clear evidence for specific outcomes in specific populations: triglyceride reduction as a biomarker effect at high doses, omega-3 index correction in low-status individuals, DHA adequacy in pregnancy, and adjunctive use in diagnosed depression with EPA-dominant preparations.

For healthy adults with adequate dietary intake, supplementation is not well-evidenced. Individual assessment of baseline status, dietary intake, life stage, and clinical context should precede any decision about supplementation and dose.

## Evidence base by outcome

### Triglyceride reduction

High-dose EPA/DHA (2-4g/day) reliably reduces fasting triglycerides by 20-30% in individuals with elevated baseline levels. This is one of the most consistent dose-dependent effects in the omega-3 literature and is replicated across multiple trial designs and populations.

Mechanistically, EPA and DHA reduce hepatic triglyceride synthesis and increase triglyceride clearance via lipoprotein lipase. The effect is well-characterised.

This should be understood as a strong biomarker effect. Triglycerides are associated with cardiovascular risk in observational data but trials that lower triglycerides via omega-3 have not consistently reduced cardiovascular events -- illustrated directly by the divergence between REDUCE-IT and STRENGTH, which used high-dose preparations in similar high-risk populations and produced opposite results on clinical endpoints. The clinical outcome relevance of omega-3-induced triglyceride reduction therefore remains uncertain.

**Evidence rating basis:** Strong biomarker effect. Dose-dependent, consistent, well-replicated. Clinical outcome relevance uncertain; outcomeType: exploratory-biomarker.

### Cardiovascular event prevention

**REDUCE-IT (Bhatt et al., NEJM 2019; n = 8,179):** 4g/day icosapentaenoic acid ethyl ester (EPA-only, Vascepa) in statin-treated patients with elevated triglycerides. Reported 25% relative risk reduction in major cardiovascular events. This is a pharmaceutical-dose EPA-only preparation, not a standard combined EPA/DHA supplement. The trial used mineral oil as placebo, which raised LDL-C and CRP in the control arm, potentially inflating apparent benefit. These methodological concerns are unresolved and limit applicability to supplement use.

**STRENGTH (Nicholls et al., JAMA 2020; n = 13,078):** 4g/day combined EPA/DHA carboxylic acids in a similar high-risk population. No reduction in cardiovascular events. The divergence from REDUCE-IT is not explained by trial heterogeneity alone -- the biological reason why EPA-only might benefit while EPA/DHA combined does not remains unresolved. This is a genuine scientific uncertainty.

**VITAL (Manson et al., NEJM 2019; n = 25,871):** 1g/day EPA/DHA in a general population cohort. Modest reduction in cardiovascular events overall, with a stronger signal in those with low fish consumption at baseline. This is the most directly applicable trial to standard supplement use.

**ORIGIN (2012; n = 12,536):** 1g/day EPA/DHA in people with dysglycaemia. No cardiovascular benefit.

**Evidence rating basis:** Moderate. Inconsistent across trials in ways that remain biologically unexplained. The most promising signal (REDUCE-IT) has unresolved methodological concerns and used a preparation not equivalent to standard supplements. A signal exists in specific subgroups (low fish intake, elevated triglycerides) but is not reliably replicated across designs.

### Omega-3 index and biomarker effects

The omega-3 index (EPA + DHA as a percentage of total red blood cell fatty acids) is the most widely used biomarker for omega-3 status. Supplementation raises the index in a dose-dependent manner. Algal DHA-dominant preparations raise DHA index reliably; EPA index is raised to a lesser degree depending on the EPA:DHA ratio.

An omega-3 index of 8% or above is associated with lower cardiovascular risk in observational data. Most Western adults without regular oily fish consumption have an index in the 4-6% range.

There are no trials showing that targeting a specific omega-3 index threshold through supplementation improves clinical outcomes. The index is a risk stratification and monitoring tool, not a validated treatment target with a demonstrated relationship to patient-important outcomes.

**outcomeType: exploratory-biomarker**

### DHA in pregnancy and early life

DHA is a structural component of the developing brain and retina. Maternal DHA status during pregnancy and lactation affects infant DHA accretion. Supplementation in women with low DHA intake has been associated with improved neurodevelopmental outcomes in offspring in some trials, though effect sizes vary and the evidence is not fully consistent.

Algal DHA is used in infant formula as a preformed DHA source. Regulatory bodies in multiple jurisdictions have accepted algal DHA for this purpose, representing meaningful institutional endorsement of its bioavailability and safety in early life.

**Evidence rating basis:** Moderate. Supports the importance of adequate DHA intake in pregnancy, particularly in those with low dietary intake. Evidence for supplementation beyond dietary adequacy is less consistent.

### Depression as adjunct treatment

Meta-analyses including Liao et al. (2019) suggest a modest benefit of omega-3 supplementation -- particularly EPA-dominant preparations -- in people with diagnosed depression, as an adjunct to standard treatment. Effect sizes are small to moderate (SMD approximately 0.3-0.5). The evidence is sensitive to study selection, publication bias, and baseline severity of depression. Pooling across different EPA:DHA ratios, doses, and populations produces heterogeneous results.

The evidence in people without a clinical diagnosis of depression is not convincing.

**Evidence rating basis:** Moderate for EPA-dominant preparations in diagnosed populations. Does not apply to general mood support in healthy adults.

### Inflammatory markers

EPA and DHA are precursors to specialised pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) including resolvins and protectins. Supplementation reduces circulating inflammatory markers including CRP, IL-6, and TNF-alpha in some populations, but effects are small and inconsistent in individuals with normal baseline inflammation. Larger and more consistent effects are seen in populations with metabolic disease or elevated baseline inflammatory markers.

**outcomeType: exploratory-biomarker**

## Algal versus fish oil: what the evidence shows

Bioavailability of algal DHA is well-established as comparable to fish oil DHA. The key reference is Arterburn et al. (2007), a randomised crossover pharmacokinetic study demonstrating equivalent DHA bioavailability from algal and fish oil sources.

Algal EPA bioavailability data is more limited but available studies are consistent with comparable absorption. Clinical outcome equivalence between algal and fish oil preparations has not been established in large trials. This is an important distinction: bioavailability equivalence does not prove clinical outcome equivalence, and no large cardiovascular or cognitive outcome trial has directly compared algal to fish oil preparations.

EPA:DHA ratios vary considerably between algal products. Some are predominantly DHA with low EPA content; others offer more balanced or EPA-enriched profiles. Given the partially distinct biological roles of EPA and DHA, evidence from one ratio profile should not be extrapolated to products with materially different compositions.

## Individual variation

**Baseline omega-3 status** is the most important moderator of response. Individuals with low omega-3 index show the greatest response to supplementation. Those with an index already in the adequate range are unlikely to benefit meaningfully from additional supplementation. Diet history provides a useful proxy for baseline status.

**Dietary intake:** Vegetarians and vegans have no dietary EPA or DHA and represent the clearest population for algal omega-3 supplementation. Omnivores consuming regular oily fish have substantially higher baseline stores.

**Life stage:** Pregnancy and lactation increase DHA requirements. Older adults may have altered fatty acid metabolism. Children and adolescents have distinct DHA accretion needs.

**Genetics:** FADS1 and FADS2 gene variants affect desaturase enzyme activity and therefore the conversion of shorter-chain ALA to EPA and DHA. Individuals with reduced FADS activity rely more heavily on preformed EPA and DHA. Routine genotyping is not recommended in current guidelines for this purpose but represents a meaningful source of biological variation.

**Cardiovascular risk profile:** The cardiovascular prevention signal is strongest in those with elevated triglycerides and established cardiovascular disease. General population supplementation without these risk factors has weaker evidential support.

## Funding and independence

The omega-3 literature includes a mixture of publicly and industry-funded work. The major cardiovascular outcome trials (REDUCE-IT, STRENGTH, VITAL, ORIGIN) were variously funded by pharmaceutical companies and public sources. REDUCE-IT was funded by Amarin, the manufacturer of Vascepa, which has been noted as a potential source of bias alongside the mineral oil placebo issue. VITAL and ORIGIN were publicly funded. Assessment of individual trials should account for funding source alongside methodology.
