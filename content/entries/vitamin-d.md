## What it is

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble vitamin that functions as a hormone in the body. It is produced in the skin on exposure to ultraviolet B radiation from sunlight, and can also be obtained from a small number of dietary sources including oily fish, egg yolks, and fortified foods.

There are two main dietary forms: vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol), derived from animal sources and produced in human skin, and vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol), derived from plant sources and fungi. D3 is more effective at raising and maintaining serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels and is the form with the strongest evidence base.

Deficiency is common. Estimates suggest that between 40 and 70 per cent of people in northern latitudes, including the UK, have insufficient vitamin D levels, particularly in winter months.

## What the evidence shows

**Deficiency correction** is where the evidence is strongest. In individuals with confirmed deficiency, vitamin D3 supplementation reliably restores serum 25(OH)D to sufficient levels and resolves associated symptoms including fatigue, bone pain, and muscle weakness. This evidence is strong, consistent, and not materially disputed.

**Immune function** has a moderate evidence base. Several well-designed RCTs show reductions in acute respiratory tract infections, particularly in individuals with low baseline vitamin D status. The effect is less consistent in replete populations. A 2017 meta-analysis in the BMJ found a protective effect, but this was stronger in those receiving daily or weekly dosing than bolus doses, and the effect was largely concentrated in deficient individuals.

**Mood and depression symptoms** show emerging evidence. A small number of RCTs suggest modest improvements in mood and depressive symptoms, but studies are generally short, effect sizes are small, and findings are inconsistent across populations. This is a promising area of research rather than an established effect.

**Cognitive performance** in generally healthy adults with normal vitamin D status has insufficient evidence. Studies are small, methodologically weak, and contradictory. Claims that vitamin D supplementation improves cognition in replete adults are not supported by the current evidence base.

## What the evidence does not show

There is no strong evidence that vitamin D supplementation prevents cancer, cardiovascular disease, or autoimmune conditions in the general population, despite earlier observational studies suggesting associations. Large well-designed RCTs including VITAL (US) and ViDA (New Zealand) found no significant reduction in major cardiovascular events or cancer incidence with supplementation in generally healthy adults.

The widely promoted cognitive benefits of vitamin D supplementation are not supported by controlled trial evidence in individuals with normal status.

## Form and dose considerations

Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is the form with the strongest evidence and is the recommended form for supplementation. D2 (ergocalciferol) is less effective at maintaining serum levels and should not be used interchangeably with D3 in clinical or supplementation contexts.

Evidence for deficiency correction typically uses doses between 1,000 and 4,000 IU per day. The dose needed to achieve and maintain adequate serum levels varies considerably between individuals based on baseline status, body weight, skin pigmentation, sun exposure, and genetic factors including VDR polymorphisms. Supplementing without knowing baseline status means you cannot determine an appropriate dose.

The upper tolerable intake level is 4,000 IU per day for most adults. Toxicity is rare at doses below this level but has been reported with sustained intake above 10,000 IU per day.

## Who the evidence applies to

The deficiency correction evidence applies most clearly to individuals with confirmed deficiency, which requires a serum 25(OH)D test. The effect of supplementation is largest in those with the lowest starting levels.

The immune function evidence applies most consistently to individuals with low baseline vitamin D status, older adults, and those with limited sun exposure. Effects in young, healthy, replete individuals are less consistent.

Genetic variation in the vitamin D receptor (VDR) gene affects individual response to supplementation and may explain some of the variability in trial outcomes.

## Safety and contraindications

Vitamin D is generally well tolerated at standard doses. The most important safety consideration is that deficiency should be confirmed before high-dose supplementation, as toxicity, while rare, can occur with sustained excessive intake.

Individuals with conditions affecting calcium metabolism, including primary hyperparathyroidism and sarcoidosis, should take medical advice before supplementing. Vitamin D interacts with some medications including thiazide diuretics. Anyone taking regular medication should check with their prescriber or pharmacist before supplementing.

## What can reasonably be concluded

Vitamin D3 supplementation is well-evidenced for correcting deficiency, and deficiency is genuinely common in northern latitudes. A serum 25(OH)D test before supplementing allows for an appropriate dose to be chosen and avoids unnecessary supplementation in those with adequate status.

The immune function evidence is moderate and appears most relevant to individuals with low baseline status. Claims extending the evidence to cognitive performance, cardiovascular protection, or cancer prevention in the general population are not well-supported by controlled trial data.

## Evidence base detail

The primary evidence base for deficiency correction includes 14 RCTs with a combined sample of approximately 3,800 participants. Risk of bias is rated low to some concerns across included trials, with the main concerns being blinding of participants in some open-label designs and variable compliance measurement. Findings are consistent in direction: D3 supplementation raises serum 25(OH)D levels reliably in deficient individuals.

The immune function evidence is more heterogeneous. The 2017 Martineau meta-analysis included 25 RCTs with over 11,000 participants and found a pooled protective effect against acute respiratory tract infections (adjusted OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.81 to 0.96). However, the effect was significantly larger in those with baseline 25(OH)D below 25 nmol/L (OR 0.70) than in those with higher starting levels (OR 0.92). This population-dependent effect size is central to how the moderate rating should be interpreted.

## Risk of bias summary

**Deficiency correction trials:** The majority of included trials are rated low to some concerns on RoB 2. The main concerns are allocation concealment in older trials and lack of blinding in open-label designs. These concerns are unlikely to materially affect the direction of findings given the objective nature of the primary outcome (serum 25(OH)D levels).

**Immune function trials:** Risk of bias is more variable. Several included trials show some concerns around randomisation and selective outcome reporting. The Martineau meta-analysis used individual participant data which partially mitigates these concerns. The heterogeneity in findings across immune outcomes (upper respiratory infections, lower respiratory infections, influenza) warrants caution in generalising.

## Funding

The majority of high-quality vitamin D trials are publicly funded. The VITAL trial was funded by the US National Institutes of Health. The Martineau meta-analysis was funded by the UK National Institute for Health Research. Commercial funding is less dominant in this area than in many supplement categories, which strengthens confidence in the overall evidence base.

## Genetic variation

VDR (vitamin D receptor) polymorphisms, particularly BsmI, ApaI, TaqI, and FokI variants, affect vitamin D metabolism and individual response to supplementation. These variants are relatively common and may partly explain the variability in trial outcomes across populations. Individuals with known VDR polymorphisms may have different requirements, though the clinical implications of routine genetic testing in this context remain under investigation.
