# Evidentia Nutrition — Editorial Standards for Contributors

This document sets out the editorial requirements that apply to all external contributions. It covers evidence standards, writing style, format requirements, and the review process. Reading it before drafting will save significant revision time.

---

## What Evidentia is

Evidentia is an independent evidence-based supplement library and commentary platform. It exists to give readers an accurate account of what the clinical evidence supports, what it does not, and what remains genuinely uncertain.

The editorial position is sceptical by default. We do not assume that a supplement works until the evidence shows otherwise. We require evidence to be judged on its quality and scope before conclusions are drawn from it. Marketing claims are not evidence.

Contributors are expected to share this orientation. Submissions that advocate for a supplement, assume clinical benefit from mechanistic data, or reach conclusions not supported by the cited evidence will not be accepted.

## Evidence standards

### The evidence hierarchy

Evidentia applies a standard evidence hierarchy. In descending order of weight:

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials carry the most weight, subject to quality assessment. A meta-analysis of poor-quality trials does not produce strong evidence.

Individual randomised controlled trials are assessed for sample size, duration, blinding, allocation concealment, and industry funding. Small trials with industry funding are treated with explicit caution.

Observational studies, cohort data, and case series can provide context and generate hypotheses. They cannot establish causation and must not be presented as though they can.

Mechanistic and preclinical evidence is used to explain proposed pathways, not to support clinical conclusions. The phrase "may help" based solely on in vitro or animal data is not an acceptable evidence claim.

### Separating mechanism from outcome

This is the most common error in supplement writing and the one we are most rigorous about.

The following are distinct and must be treated as such in all submissions:

A proposed biological mechanism describes how a compound might plausibly produce an effect. It does not demonstrate that the effect occurs in humans at supplemental doses.

A biomarker change describes a measurable laboratory shift, for example a reduction in a blood marker. It is not equivalent to a clinical outcome unless the biomarker has been validated as a surrogate for that outcome in the relevant population.

A clinical outcome describes a meaningful change in health, function, or disease course in humans, demonstrated in a controlled setting.

Submissions must maintain these distinctions throughout. A sentence such as "compound X reduces inflammation, making it useful for joint pain" is not acceptable unless randomised trial evidence in a joint pain population directly supports both the inflammation claim and the clinical outcome.

On the role of mechanistic evidence: it has a legitimate place in submissions, but only a defined one. Mechanistic evidence may be used to explain why a clinical effect is biologically plausible, to contextualise the pathway through which an observed effect might operate, or to frame a research gap. It may not be used to argue that a clinical effect is probable, likely, or likely to be confirmed by future trials. When mechanistic evidence is the only evidence available, the correct conclusion is that no human outcome data exists, not that the compound shows promise. Where mechanistic data conflicts with null trial results, the trial results take precedence.

### Evidence ratings

All library entries carry a primary evidence rating and ratings for individual outcome areas. The ratings are defined below, including the operational criteria that must be met. These thresholds are not targets to be reached with selective citation; they describe what the full evidence base must support.

**Strong** requires at least three independent randomised controlled trials with low risk of bias, conducted by different research groups, showing consistent direction of effect, with effect sizes and target populations well-characterised across trials. Strong ratings always carry a scope limitation specifying the population and outcome the rating applies to. No supplement receives a blanket Strong rating.

**Moderate** applies where at least one randomised controlled trial exists but the evidence base has meaningful limitations. These include inconsistent replication, small sample sizes, short trial duration, single research group, or evidence that is restricted to a narrowly defined population. The rating acknowledges that a signal exists but that significant uncertainty remains about its magnitude, generalisability, or durability.

**Emerging** applies where the evidence is limited to one or two trials, where available trials have a high risk of bias, where sample sizes are small (typically fewer than 50 participants per arm), or where findings have not been replicated outside the originating research group. Emerging does not imply that the evidence is promising or that positive results are expected; it indicates that no reliable conclusion can yet be drawn.

**Insufficient** applies where no randomised controlled trial evidence exists, where available evidence is conflicting with no discernible signal, or where the evidence base consists entirely of preclinical or mechanistic work. Insufficient is not a judgement on the compound's potential; it is a description of the current state of the evidence.

When uncertain between two ratings, apply the lower one and state the reason explicitly in the submission.

### Downgrading for risk of bias

Assigning a rating is not the final step. The rating must then be assessed against the risk-of-bias profile of the supporting evidence. The following require a mandatory downgrade of one level, or an explicit justification for not downgrading:

A high risk of bias assessment on the primary supporting trials, including inadequate blinding, allocation concealment failures, or high attrition without adequate handling. Industry funding of the primary supporting trials, where no independent replication exists. All supporting evidence originating from a single research group with no independent replication. Primary trial sample sizes below 50 participants per arm across the evidence base.

Where multiple downgrade factors apply simultaneously, the rating should be considered for a two-level downgrade. Where a contributor believes the evidence warrants resisting a downgrade despite one of these factors, the case must be made explicitly in the IN DEPTH section.

### Effect size reporting

Outcome claims must include quantitative effect size data where available. Stating that a supplement "significantly reduced" a marker or "improved" an outcome without specifying the magnitude is not acceptable. Where effect sizes are reported in source trials, they must be included in the submission with the appropriate unit and confidence interval. Where a trial reports statistical significance without a clinically meaningful effect size, this discrepancy must be addressed explicitly.

Where effect size data is genuinely unavailable in the source literature, this absence must be stated directly rather than worked around with qualitative language. Absolute risk changes are preferred over relative risk where both are available. Practical significance should be discussed separately from statistical significance.

### Industry funding

All industry-funded trials must be flagged in the submission. The specific funder must be named. The directional consequence of funder bias (typically effect size inflation) must be acknowledged. This is not optional.

Submissions that cite industry-funded trials as though they were independent evidence will be returned for revision.

### Reference verification

Every reference cited in a submission must be verified against the primary source before submission. This means:

The DOI must resolve to the correct paper. Author names, year, journal, and sample size must match what is stated in the body of the submission. The paper must directly support the specific claim it is cited for.

We do not accept submissions where references have been generated or assumed rather than checked. Any reference that cannot be verified should be removed before submission.

References must be formatted in Harvard style with the full DOI included. Where no DOI exists, include the direct URL to the primary source.

Preprints must be labelled as such and cannot serve as primary support for major clinical claims.

## Writing style

### Prose only

All body text must be written in full prose paragraphs. No bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold subheadings within body sections. Headers use H2 level only.

This is not a stylistic preference. Bullet-pointed supplement content is the format of marketing copy. Prose forces precision: you cannot hide a weak claim in a bullet point the way you can in a sentence that must logically connect to the one before it.

### Calibrated language

Use language that accurately reflects evidential uncertainty. The following are preferred:

modest, limited, inconsistent, preliminary, suggestive, not established, not demonstrated in humans, restricted to deficiency populations, based on biomarker data only.

The following are not acceptable in Evidentia content:

proven, boosts, enhances (unless referring to a specific quantified change in a specific population), notable signal, compelling evidence (unless the evidence genuinely is), defensible use case, shows promise (this is marketing language).

### Scope precision

Evidence almost always has a defined scope. State it. "Magnesium supplementation improves sleep" is not acceptable. "Magnesium supplementation reduced sleep onset latency in older adults with low dietary magnesium intake, based on two small randomised controlled trials" is acceptable.

Deficiency populations and replete populations must be distinguished. Most nutrients show meaningful effects primarily or exclusively in deficient populations. Do not generalise findings from deficient populations to general supplementation.

Female life stages must be addressed wherever relevant evidence exists. This includes perimenopause, menopause, pregnancy, and fertility. Submissions that present only male or mixed-population data as though it applies universally will be returned for this to be addressed.

### Subgroup findings

Subgroup analyses require explicit caution. When a trial reports a significant finding in a subgroup that was not the prespecified primary endpoint, this must be flagged as a hypothesis-generating finding with an elevated false-positive risk, not treated as evidence of efficacy in that subgroup.

### Tone

The tone should be analytical and neutral. We are not writing for an audience that wants to be told what to take. We are writing for an audience that wants to understand what the evidence actually shows.

Avoid advocacy. Avoid enthusiasm about a supplement's potential. Avoid framing that implies you are on the supplement's side. A good test: would a clinical pharmacologist reviewing this content find anything to object to in the framing?

## Format requirements

### Library entries

Library entries follow a fixed section structure that cannot be modified. The required order is:

**What it is** covers the biological role, relevant forms or strains, and any context necessary to frame the evidence discussion. Two to four paragraphs.

**What the evidence shows** summarises the overall evidence landscape, key limitations, and the overall rating. This section should allow a reader to understand the state of the evidence without reading further.

**Five questions** addresses each of the following in turn: Does low status cause harm? Does supplementation prevent disease? Does it affect biomarkers? Does it help clinical populations? Does it benefit healthy individuals? Each question must be answered based on the available evidence.

**Individual variation** covers population differences relevant to the supplement, including sex, age, baseline status, dietary pattern, and genetic variation where evidence exists.

**Testing and status assessment** explains what testing is available, what it measures, and its limitations. Where no validated test exists, this must be stated.

**Safety** covers adverse effects, contraindications, drug interactions, and pregnancy considerations. This section is mandatory even where the safety profile appears benign.

**What can reasonably be concluded** provides a calibrated summary. This section must end with the following standard sentence, verbatim: "Where evidence is limited or outcomes are uncertain, conclusions should be treated as provisional and subject to revision as the evidence base develops."

**IN DEPTH** follows a horizontal rule divider and provides detailed trial-level analysis with effect sizes, population characterisation, and the basis for each evidence rating assigned. References appear in this section.

### Articles

Articles follow no fixed template. The opening paragraph appears before the first H2 header with no heading above it. Citations are woven into the body text by author, year, and journal. A Key references section at the end lists full citations for named studies.

Articles must close with an italicised footer line cross-linking to relevant library entries.

Articles do not include a five-questions section, an IN DEPTH section, or a testing section.

### Frontmatter

Library entry frontmatter and article frontmatter follow fixed schemas. These will be provided to contributors invited to submit a full draft. All YAML date fields must be formatted as quoted strings.

## Conflict of interest policy

All contributors must submit a conflict of interest declaration alongside their draft. The declaration must cover:

Any financial relationship with supplement manufacturers, distributors, or retailers, including consultancy, advisory roles, speaker fees, or equity holdings.

Any research funding from industry sources relevant to the topic of the submission, regardless of whether the contributor was the principal investigator.

Any personal use or advocacy for the supplement or ingredient being reviewed that could reasonably affect objectivity.

The declaration is published alongside the contribution. If no conflicts exist, a brief statement to that effect is published.

Submissions without a COI declaration will not be reviewed. Undisclosed conflicts discovered after publication will result in a correction notice and may result in retraction.

## Authorship and editorial control

Named authorship with full credentials appears on all accepted contributions. Contributors retain the right to share links to their published work and to cite the piece in professional contexts.

Evidentia retains editorial control of the published version. This includes the right to make minor factual corrections, update references as new evidence emerges, and apply style edits to ensure consistency with the platform's format. Substantive changes will always be discussed with the author before publication.

Authors may request corrections to published work by contacting contact@evidentianutrition.org. We will review and respond within three weeks.

## What the review process looks like in practice

Proposals are assessed for topic fit, scope overlap with existing content, and whether the proposed argument is editorially appropriate. We respond to all proposals within two weeks.

Invited full drafts undergo editorial review focused on evidence calibration, factual accuracy, reference verification, and adherence to the format and style requirements. We will return specific, detailed feedback rather than general comments. Submissions must include the completed contributor checklist from the end of this document; drafts submitted without it will be returned unreviewed.

Specialist submissions may be sent for independent peer review, particularly for clinical topics or where the contributor has a declared interest. Reviewers are asked to assess factual accuracy and evidence calibration, not style.

Revision is typically one to two rounds. If a submission requires more than two rounds of significant revision, we will have a direct conversation about whether it is the right fit.

We do not publish contributions that we cannot editorially stand behind. If a submission is declined after a full draft is submitted, we will explain the specific reason.

## Quick checklist for contributors

Complete this checklist and include it with your draft submission. Drafts submitted without it will be returned.

Every reference has been verified against the primary source, with DOI confirmed as resolving correctly and claim-to-source match confirmed.

Mechanism, biomarker change, and clinical outcome are consistently distinguished throughout. Mechanistic evidence is used only to explain a proposed pathway, not to support a clinical conclusion.

Evidence ratings are applied conservatively and meet the operational thresholds defined above. Where a downgrade factor applies, it has either been applied or explicitly argued against in the IN DEPTH section.

Effect sizes are reported with units and confidence intervals wherever available in the source literature. Where unavailable, the absence is stated directly.

Industry-funded trials are flagged by funder name, and the directional consequence of that funding is acknowledged.

Deficiency and replete populations are distinguished where relevant.

Female life stages are addressed where relevant evidence exists.

Subgroup findings are flagged as hypothesis-generating with elevated false-positive risk where they were not prespecified primary endpoints.

No bullet points or numbered lists appear in the body text.

A conflict of interest declaration is included with the submission.

The draft has been read for overstatement before sending. Any claim that goes beyond what the directly cited evidence supports has been identified and either corrected or explicitly hedged.
